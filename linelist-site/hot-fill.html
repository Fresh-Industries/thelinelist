Hot Fill Co-Packers for Sauces, Juices, Teas, and Shots

Last verified 21 Aug 2026 · Search volume: unpublished · Named plants only · MOQ only when the plant printed one

Use hot fill when high-acid juice, tea, shot, or sauce can take 185–200°F and must be ambient. Heat plus acidity is the kill step. You do not need a retort shell.

Do not send never-cooked or probiotic juice here (that is HPP). Do not send low-acid ambient broth or plant milk here (pH greater than 4.6 is LACF — retort or aseptic). Dairy is plant-specific: Yoshida refuses it; InnoMark does not produce high-pH refrigerated products.

How to read the MOQ column

Published minimums are not comparable units. InnoMark quotes liters, Creative Foodworks gallons per batch, Big Brands units per SKU, Yoshida a gallons-per-day annual screen, Paradise a typical project start. Convert before you rank plants. A 7,500 L InnoMark run is not the same commercial commitment as Yoshida’s 11,000 gal/day × 5 days/year screen.

If MOQ is unpublished, assume nothing.

Textbook — after the shortlist

What hot fill actually is

Hot fill is a thermal fill, not a pressure cook. The product is heated, filled hot into a heat-capable container, closed, and held so the heat sanitizes the product, the headspace, and the closure. The pack is then cooled. As it cools, the headspace contracts and a vacuum forms.

Two plants publish fill temperatures on their own sites:

  • InnoMark: product heated to 185–200°F, filled, sealed, inverted to sanitize the cap, then cooled.
  • Big Brands: product heated to 194°F, filled into glass or PET, held 15–20 seconds, then cooled (their page also describes a rest to 180°F before cap — confirm the actual line sequence in a plant walkthrough).

Hot fill is the default ambient process for high-acid juices, teas, sports drinks, wellness shots, ketchup-style sauces, hot sauce, and many marinades. Heat plus acidity is the kill step. You do not need a retort shell or an aseptic room to make those SKUs shelf-stable.

It is the wrong process when heat destroys the product you are selling, or when acidity cannot carry the safety case.

When hot fill is the wrong process

HPP-sensitive products. Cold-pressed juice, guacamole, salsa sold on “never cooked,” live-culture drinks, and anything whose label story is raw or probiotic will not survive 185–200°F. Send those to an HPP or cold-fill + refrigerate plant, not a hot-fill house. Select Juice, Maryland Packaging, and Big Brands also publish HPP as a separate process — do not assume a plant that lists both will hot-fill a juice you intended for HPP.

Many dairy SKUs. Milk, creamers, yogurt drinks, and dairy-containing sauces are usually low-acid. Heat also cooks dairy proteins. Yoshida’s hot-fill inquiry refuses dairy outright because the Portland plant is Kosher Pareve. InnoMark’s FAQ says it does not produce “high pH refrigerated products.” If your formula has dairy, ask the plant for a dairy allergen program and a process authority letter before you send a sample.

Low-acid products that need a scheduled retort (or aseptic) process. FDA’s acidified / low-acid canned food framework (21 CFR 108 / 113 / 114) is the line, not a plant’s marketing list. A food with finished equilibrium pH greater than 4.6 and water activity greater than 0.85, packed in a hermetically sealed container for ambient sale, is a low-acid canned food. That class needs a filed scheduled process — typically retort or aseptic — not a sauce hot-fill line. Broths, plant milks, protein drinks, and cheese sauces often sit here even when a plant lists “broths” next to juice. If pH will not equilibrate at or below 4.6, do not force hot fill.

Carbonation and alcohol (plant-specific). InnoMark’s FAQ: they do not produce carbonated or alcohol products. Big Brands’ hot-fill page does list alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages. Match the plant to the SKU; do not assume every hot-fill house will run a spiked tea.

Acid vs low-acid (operator version)

Use the FDA definitions, then make the plant prove they have a process authority letter for your formula.

Class Finished equilibrium pH Water activity What the plant must have Typical process
Acid food (naturally acid) 4.6 or below without added acid usually > 0.85 Thermal process sufficient to kill vegetative pathogens and spoilage organisms that can grow in the pack Hot fill is common (juice, many fruit sauces)
Acidified food 4.6 or below after acid is added to a low-acid food > 0.85 Establishment registration + filed scheduled process (Form FDA 2541e) under 21 CFR 108/114; pH control as a critical factor Hot fill is common if the formula acidifies in time
Low-acid canned food (LACF) greater than 4.6 > 0.85 Establishment registration + filed scheduled process under 21 CFR 108/113 Retort, aseptic — not a standard hot-fill acid line
Refrigerated (not ambient) any any Cold chain; LACF/AF filing often does not apply if the product is stored, distributed, and retailed under refrigeration Cold fill, HPP, or hot fill then refrigerate — plant-specific

FDA’s public summary: LACF excludes alcoholic beverages and tomatoes/tomato products with finished equilibrium pH less than 4.7. Tomato SKUs sit in that special band — do not assume a salsa is “just acid.” Get a process authority to classify it.

USDA vs FDA is a different cut. Meat- or poultry-containing sauces can fall under USDA FSIS, not FDA, depending on meat content. None of the plants in the table below publish a clear “USDA-inspected meat line vs FDA juice line” split on the pages fetched. Treat that column as unpublished unless the plant states FDA-registered or USDA Organic (organic is not the same as FSIS meat inspection).

PET vs glass vs pouch

Hot fill needs a package that survives fill temperature. Confirm hot-fill-grade PET, not a cold-fill water bottle. A “pouch” plant may mean retort pouch — ask which.
